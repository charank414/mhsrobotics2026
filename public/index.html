The job

BEST Robotics is opening a restaurant. Your team is the vendor that pitched a robot to run it, and Game Day is the demo. Everything on this page comes from the official v3.2 rulebook released 2 September 2026.

The field is the Byte to Bite Restaurant: four mirrored quadrants around a shared central dining room. Each quadrant belongs to one team, colored yellow, blue, red or green. The two quadrants on a half of the field share their gardens and compete for the ingredients in them. The two quadrants on opposite halves share a Balance Buffet and have to cooperate to score on it.

Across three minutes your robot has to do five things:

  • Gather ingredients and build meals on a plate
  • Carry meals safely to one of the scoring areas
  • Spin the water valve to irrigate your garden
  • Twist a handle to send the crop-tending drone across the garden
  • Enter the dining room under its own control and place a meal on a table

Meanwhile a second student â€” the spotter â€” can build meals by hand from whatever the robot ferries back to the start area. It's a two-person game.

How a match works

Four robots, one field, three minutes. Points are counted at the end, not as you go.

Harvest

Pull ingredients from the five garden stations. Every ingredient has a hole in the middle â€” that hole is how meals get built.

Plate

Thread ingredients down the stem of your team-colored plate. What you stack, and in what order, decides whether it's a salad, a pizza or a sandwich.

Deliver

Warming shelf, balance buffet, dining room table, quality check area, or just hold it. Each spot is worth a different bonus.

Tend

Open the irrigation valve, run the drone down its rope, and empty every station for the harvest bonuses.

The rule everyone forgets

Ingredients score nothing on their own. A pile of eight vegetables on your robot at the buzzer is worth zero. Only completed meals, filled drinks and field tasks put points on the card.

Where the points are

Two ways to score a meal, and they pay differently. The robot route is worth roughly double the spotter route â€” but the spotter can work while the robot is still out on the field.

The season

Eight weeks from kickoff to Game Day. Two of these events are required; the rest depend on your hub.

Week 0 · required

Kickoff

Kits are handed out, the game is revealed, and the eight-week clock starts. No robot part may be built before this day, and no parts may be reused from a previous season.

Week 2 · optional

Preliminary Design Review

A two-way conversation with hub mentors once you've digested the requirements, brainstormed, drafted a schedule and picked your subsystems. Check whether your hub offers it.

Week 5 · required

Critical Design Review

Mandatory for hubs to offer. An overview of the design so far, focused on risks, alternatives and opportunities while there's still time to recover. Worth 5 points on the engineering notebook scoresheet.

Week 6 · hub's call

Practice Day

Time on the actual physical field you'll compete on. Hubs set the requirements and may exempt a team.

Week 8 · required

Game Day

One or two days of head-to-head competition, judging, presentations and exhibition.

Two deadlines that sit before Game Day

The engineering notebook is due one week before the game competition â€” miss it and the team is ineligible to compete. The digital marketing video is due at least seven days before Game Day. Every student on the roster must also complete the Student Participation Survey, which is verified at the robot compliance check.

The Byte to Bite field

Four quadrants mirrored around a central dining room. Each half of the field is shared by two neighbouring teams who compete for the same garden ingredients. Teams in opposite halves share a Balance Buffet and only score it if both sides cooperate.

The garden, station by station

The field splits into two halves along the line running through the warming shelves and balance buffets. Each half has one Garden Area, running along a dining room wall and divided into two mirrored halves â€” one designated to each of that half’s two teams. A garden half holds 44 game pieces; the whole Garden Area holds 88, and every one of them is fair game for either team on that half of the field, regardless of colour.

Vegetable Tray

A slanted hydroponic flood tray holding green disks, loaded vertically in three rows of two. Release them with the button at the bottom of the tray, or just pull them straight out â€” both count.

6 per garden half · 24 per field · harvest bonus 4 pts

Fruit Vines

Red balls hung on three velcro vines strung across a PVC frame. Three start in a row low on the vines, one starts higher on the centre vine, and two start on the floor in front, touching tape markers.

6 per garden half · 24 per field · harvest bonus 4 pts

Cold Food Storage Fridge

Dairy â€” yellow square slabs â€” sit in two stacks of three on the lower shelf, on the handle side. Protein â€” brown round disks â€” sit in two stacks of three on the upper shelf, on the hinge side. Open the door, then pull them out.

That combines two descriptions the rulebook gives differently — 3.3.2.1.C puts both ingredients on both shelves. See Open Questions.

6 dairy + 6 protein per garden half · harvest bonus 8 pts for clearing both

Breadbox

White bread-shaped grain squares, stored along the dining room wall at the outer end of the garden. Two slices start on top of the box. Grain resting on the breadbox does not score, and the harvest bonus needs those top slices gone too.

12 per garden half · 48 per field · harvest bonus 8 pts

Water Station

Eight blue tennis balls sit in an angled track above the fruit vines. Rotate the T-handle a quarter turn â€” either direction, horizontal to vertical â€” and the water rolls out the front. Reaching into the station or the irrigation pipe to grab water is a disqualification.

8 per garden half · valve release 4 pts to the owning team

Crop-Tending Drone

Rotate the T-handle on the drone post â€” either direction â€” to drag the drone along its support rope. It scores once the front edge of the wooden body crosses the line marked on top of the garden wall. Touching the drone, the support rope or the pull rope directly is not allowed.

1 per team · 8 pts, the biggest single field task

Garden tasks are owned, not earned

The drone, the irrigation valve and all four harvest bonuses score for the team that owns that field structure â€” the one whose Robot Start Area is closest â€” no matter which of the two robots on that half actually does the work. Your neighbour can empty the breadbox and hand you 8 points, and you can do the same for them.

The dining room is the only autonomous zone

Nine tables in a 3×3 array behind four pinwheeled walls, with a doorway at each corner and a strict rule about who’s driving.

The four walls are offset in a pinwheel so the way in is at each corner of the room. Two of those four doorways lead to your half of the field, and those are the only two you may leave through. A robot may only enter the dining room under autonomous control while the driver is hands-free â€” meaning the V5 Controller is out of the driver's hands and they have no manual control of the machine. Every scoring task inside the room has to be done autonomously. Placing a meal on a table under remote control is a disqualification.

To help, printed fiducials from the AprilTag Circle21h7 family are placed on the unpainted dining room walls and on the table tops. The V5 AI Vision Sensor can read them, and each unique pattern of black and white modules encodes a different numeric ID so the robot knows which one it's looking at.

A meal counts as scored the moment the autonomous robot has placed the dish and is no longer touching it. The plate must completely cover the centre 4¼″ white square of the table's fiducial. One meal per table â€” a second meal on an occupied table earns a 20-second penalty and scores nothing.

Four ways to end an autonomous run

  • Place the meal, back off, and drive out manually once you're clear
  • Abandon the attempt at any time and drive out manually
  • Exit through one of the two doorways leading to your half of the field â€” the other two are a DQ
  • Leave it there. Nothing forces you to retrieve the robot

Never touch what's already served

Robots may not remove, dislodge or control any game piece in the dining room. Pieces that were in a scoring position before a violation still count as scored â€” but nothing on the field is ever reset mid-match, and each game piece can score only once.

The Balance Buffet

The one scoring area you cannot win alone. Drag the slider to see what the referee is looking at.

In balance â€” meals on both sides score

The Balance Buffet sits between the Warming Shelf and the dining room, straddling the wall that splits the field into halves. Each buffet is shared with one team from the opposite half, and each of you may only touch your own colour-designated side.

It's balanced when the 1×4 dividing wall of the rotating platform falls fully between the two tape indicators on the support post. If that divider crosses the inner edge of either tape line, the buffet is out of balance and nothing on it scores â€” for either of you.

That's 15 points per meal, up to three meals, and it only pays if your counterpart on the far side of the field loads their side to match. Interacting with your own designated shelf is never treated as de-scoring their dish, so you can add weight; touching their shelf is a disqualification.

Outside the boundary: the human areas

Four zones sit outside the field wall at each quadrant, and the traffic between them is tightly regulated.

Driver Box

One student, feet inside the box for the whole match. Leaving it costs 20 seconds. The driver may not touch the field, a game piece, or the robot â€” that's a DQ.

Spotter Box

One student, free to move within the area, with access to the Robot Start Area. Any spotter from the team may take the role â€” unlike the driver, there's no rotation.

Robot Start Area

Taped box, measured from the outside edge of the tape. The robot starts fully inside it in its starting configuration. It doubles as the handoff point where the spotter collects pieces.

Quality Check Area

Taped area on the floor beside the spotter box. Meals the spotter assembles are placed here. Plates may not overlap or cross the outside of the tape lines.

The handoff protocol

The robot drops ingredients and dishware into the RSA. The spotter may only reach in when the robot is completely outside the RSA, the driver is hands-free, or both â€” the rulebook words this condition three different ways, so build for the strict reading and see Open Questions. Anything the spotter touches must end up in the Quality Check Area â€” it can't be left in the RSA and it can't go back onto the field. Meals in the QCA can be taken apart and rebuilt, but nothing ever returns to play. Loose ingredients left in the QCA at the end of the match score nothing, and neither does water outside a cup.

The three meals

Every ingredient has a hole through the middle. For a piece to count, the plate's stem must pass through that hole â€” an ingredient merely resting on the plate is not part of the meal.

Salad · 10 pts

Any two different types of ingredient, stacked in any order. The cheapest meal to make and the fastest â€” a vegetable and a fruit is a legal salad.

Pizza · 30 pts

Grain on the bottom, then at least three different ingredient types above it. Only the grain's position matters; the toppings can go on in any order.

Sandwich · 50 pts

Grain on the bottom and grain on top, with four different ingredient types in between. Grain is the only ingredient whose position matters.

Types, not pieces

The count that decides a meal is the number of types of ingredient, not how many pieces there are. Two dairy slices on a plate count together as one type. Since there are only five types â€” grain, dairy, protein, vegetable, fruit â€” a sandwich needs grain plus all four of the others.

Drinks

A cup with at least one water ball in it. An empty cup scores nothing, and a loose water ball scores nothing.

A drink scores on its own if it's upright with the base of the cup flat on the floor or a shelf, or held upright by the robot. It can't score on its own in the dining room.

Added to a meal it's worth far more. Place the cup upright on the plate, base in contact with and fully supported by the plate â€” before or after the plate reaches its scoring area, either works. The cup colour and plate colour must match, and at most one drink per meal scores.

The two students on the floor

One driver, one spotter, and a rulebook that spends more words on their interaction than on almost anything else.

Driver

Only one student per match may operate the V5 Controller. Adult coaches and teachers are not allowed in the driver or spotter area during matches, and students may not stand on platforms of their own construction â€” or on each other â€” to get a better view. Hubs can make provisions for special-needs drivers case by case.

At registration each team receives up to eight wristbands numbered 1 to 8. Coaches fill out a driver roster on competition day naming which student wears which number, and the bands are not transferable.

Driver 1 drives match one, driver 2 drives match two, and so on until the numbers run out and the rotation restarts at 1. If a student can't compete in a round their number is skipped and they rejoin in their numbered position later. The rotation carries across phases â€” seeding into semi-finals into finals â€” picking up where the last phase left off. If organisers cancel a match, the driver scheduled for it drives the next one.

Spotter

Any student on the team may spot; there is no rotation. The spotter moves freely inside the spotter box and has access to the Robot Start Area.

Before the robot leaves the RSA for the first time, the spotter may touch the robot to fix power, software or radio problems. The permitted actions are exactly four:

  • Plug or unplug the VEX V5 battery
  • Turn the V5 brain off or on
  • Plug or unplug the V5 radio
  • Use the brain's touch screen to change software settings or the running program

After that first exit, the spotter may not touch the robot at all. Specifically, they may not move parts or reposition components to help unload game pieces.

No signalling

During a match, drivers and spotters may not communicate with anyone through electronic devices or any signalling aid that isn't part of the human body â€” no signs, sticks or marked gloves. Earbuds are banned on the game floor unless a medical need has been cleared in advance with the head referee, team advocate, pit boss or hub personnel. Spotters and drivers may not handle game pieces before a match.

Match protocol

What happens in the two minutes before the buzzer and the minute after it.

Before

  • At least five referees work each match: the head referee keeps time, four watch one team each.
  • Teams learn their field and position assignment at least two minutes before the match and must be in staging by the end of the previous match.
  • Wait in staging until the setup period is signalled, then place the robot in the assigned start position.
  • You may set the robot down and power it on once you reach your quadrant. The spotter or driver may enter the field to place and prepare it; extra team members may help but must leave before the start.
  • Temporary alignment marks on the field are not permitted.
  • Miss the setup deadline and the head referee may let you keep placing it, with a 20-second penalty applied at the start of the match.

After

  • The driver and spotter stay in their designated areas until referees finish scoring and say robots may be removed.
  • The referee reviews the scored items with the driver, who signs the scorecard to confirm agreement.
  • A team-provided translator â€” an adult or a team member â€” may join that conversation.
  • Referees have ultimate authority and no protests are allowed. Questions go to a Team Advocate, never to referees, scorekeepers or other officials.

A referee may untangle machines caught on the field or each other for more than 10 seconds, or where the entanglement looks likely to damage the field, and may tell a driver to stop trying to free their machine. Being high-centred on a field element or a game piece is not entanglement. Neither is being tipped over.

Four phases of Game Day

The same structure at hubs and at championships.

SeedingRound robin, up to 8 matches per team against random opponents. Never fewer than 5, and every team plays the same number. Ranking is the average of match scores with each team's lowest score dropped.
WildcardOne extra shot for strong notebooks. With 32 or fewer teams, the 4 highest notebook scores among teams that haven't advanced play one match and the winner takes the last semi-final slot. Above 32 teams, 8 teams play 2 matches and the top 2 advance.
Semi-finals8 teams (or 16 at larger events), each playing 3 matches on a fixed rotation. Ranking is the total of those three matches â€” nothing dropped, and all earlier scores are discarded. Top 4 advance.
FinalsThe top 4 teams play 4 matches, rotating through all four quadrants. Total points across those 4 matches decides the winner. Again, no scores dropped and earlier phases disregarded.
Ties

Where the game rules don't name a tiebreaker, the default order is: robot performance game score, then engineering notebook score, then digital marketing video score.

Match scheduling tries to give every team a turn on each quadrant and to limit back-to-back matches. Any schedule change has to be communicated to the teams.

Resetting the field between matches

Worth knowing because it tells you exactly what "start of match" looks like.

Garden stations

  • Vegetable tray: slide the button/row stop to line the stops up with the slots, then reload the disks into the frame.
  • Fruit vines: four fruit rehung at the marked spots, two placed on the tape below the station.
  • Fridge: dairy restacked on the bottom shelf in two stacks of three, protein on the top shelf in two stacks of three, door closed.
  • Breadbox: grain restacked with the flat edge of the slice toward the front, two slices on top, handle closed.
  • Water station: dispenser emptied, valve back to horizontal, 8 blue tennis balls loaded into the track above the fruit vines.
  • Drone: returned to the side above the water dispenser, rope and handle reset to horizontal.

Scoring areas and dishware

  • Dining room: tables cleared so the fiducials are visible, fiducials checked for damage, tables checked for position.
  • Warming shelves: meals removed.
  • Balance buffet: meals removed, buffet set to neutral.
  • Dishware: collected from the field and sorted back by colour. Plates in two rows of four, not overlapping, not past the outer edge of the taped area. Cups upright in a single row of eight along the field boundary, bases touching the tape line.

Penalties and disqualifications

Two consequences exist. Filter by which one you're worried about.

Disqualification

No points are awarded for that match and the match cannot be re-run. Referees will DQ a team for a major breach of the rules, and their decisions are final.

20-second penalty

The driver hands the V5 Controller to a referee for 20 seconds, then gets it back and play continues. Assessed once per occurrence â€” repeat the infraction and you pay again.

A third outcome: out of play

Not every mistake is a penalty. Under rule 3.6.2.A, any game piece that touches the floor outside the field boundary — or that is placed in, or falls into, the spotter box — is simply considered out of play and is removed by the referee. It costs you no points and no time, but that piece is gone for the rest of the match. The one exception is the game pieces, completed meals and drinks the spotter deliberately places into the Quality Check Area, which stay in play for scoring.

Playing alongside three other robots

The point of the design is that four machines share limited game pieces and limited scoring spots. These rules keep that competitive rather than destructive.

Strategies aimed purely at destruction, damage, over-turning or entangling an opponent's machine are outside the spirit of the competition and are not allowed. Turning an opponent over may or may not draw a penalty, at the referees' discretion.

Machines must operate by reacting only against the surfaces of the playing field â€” including PVC pipes and ramps â€” and the air. Clamping to anything in the field is allowed; clamping to another machine is not.

Gaining traction or gripping game pieces with adhesives, or by abrading or breaking the field surface, is not allowed. The friction tape from the consumable kit is not treated as an adhesive and is specifically intended for gripping. Spiked wheels are legal only if the part touching the field is more than ¼″ in width or depth.

Detachable components

A component counts as detached once no kit part connects it, directly or indirectly, to the set of parts that includes the battery. Detached parts may be used to capture, contain or manipulate game pieces, and to block another machine.

They may not be launched at, deliberately attached to, or used to entangle another machine. Incidental contact between a machine and a deployed detachable part earns nobody a penalty, and detached components don't count as "part of the machine" unless a rule says so.

Projectiles

Anything launched through the air, free-flying or tethered, must have a frontal area greater than 10 square inches. Parts that fall off and stay on the playing surface are not projectiles.

Safety

Not negotiable, and enforced in the pit as well as on the field.

Tools in the pit

No power tools, including battery-operated ones, with two exceptions: a power drill/driver and an electrically powered soldering iron or gun. Common hand tools are fine.

The drill/driver may be used for drilling and for inserting or removing hardware â€” not for grinding, sawing or routing. Allowed power tools may only be run in the pit area or a hub-designated workstation. Illegal tools may be confiscated for the day.

Your pit space

  • Each team gets its own workspace in the pit. If the hub doesn't provide a table, you may bring one with a surface area no greater than 2,400 square inches.
  • One electrical plug per team for battery charging. Exact specifications and location vary by hub.
  • You may bring a toolbox of basic hand tools, subject to the safety constraints above.
  • Break a part you can't repair with what you have, and the sponsoring BEST organisation will make its best effort to help you replace it from local shops or spare material.
  • At least one Practice Day or Mall Day runs before Game Day. Tables and electricity are shared, teams bring their own tools, and the same safety rules apply.

On the machine and around it

  • Batteries, chargers and other BEST Control System Kit components may not be tampered with or altered in any way.
  • Anyone working on the machine in the pit wears safety gear appropriate to the task â€” safety glasses for soldering or drilling, for instance.
  • Referees and compliance officials will disqualify any machine that looks like a hazard to the field, other robots or people. The robot itself should be safe to handle.
  • Everyone on the gym floor wears shoes appropriate to the surface, as determined by the sponsoring BEST organisation.

Definitions that decide calls

Section 3.2.1. Worth reading before you argue with a referee â€” which, incidentally, you may not do.

Resting on

Touching such that the entire weight of the object is supported by what it rests on.

Inside

Within the imaginary infinite planes defined by the innermost sides of a container or area, or the outer edge of a tape line. Planes run normal to the bottom of the area.

Covered

The object cannot be seen without moving something else.

Control

Pulling, dragging, lifting or carrying a game piece. Pushing it does not count as control.

Hands-free

The V5 Controller is not in the driver's hands and the driver has no remote control of the robot.

Remote control

The driver has manual control of the robot via the V5 Controller. The opposite of hands-free — and the state the dining room forbids.

Designated

The field structure closest to a coloured Robot Start Area is owned by that team.

Drive / climb on

The robot is using part of the field structure to gain leverage or advantageous positioning.

Field structure

Any physical part of the field built to stabilise the field, the game elements or the border.

Balance

What you may and may not modify

The returnable kit goes back to the hub at the end of the season in the condition you received it. The consumable kit is yours to cut up.

Returnable kit â€” hands off

  • No modification, with three exceptions: belt stock may be cut and punched (the pre-made loop belt may not), servo horns may be modified as desired, and the BEST IR Sensor Kit may be assembled.
  • Motors and servos may never be opened â€” no re-gearing, no re-winding.
  • Pulleys, bearings and the shoulder screw may not be modified, glued or taped.
  • No tape, adhesive or glue on any returnable item unless a rule specifically allows it. The adhesive side of the hook-and-loop fastener may not go on the battery or any returnable item.
  • No paint on any returnable item.
  • The V5 Controller, servos, brain, AI Vision Sensor, batteries and chargers may not be tampered with, modified or adjusted. Only exception: the brain and AI sensor may be programmed freely.
  • No labels or rubber bands on the V5 Controller.
  • No soldering to the brain or to any interfacing cable.
  • Residue-free painters tape from the consumable kit may go on returnable items â€” except the V5 Controller.

All VEX licensed products â€” brain, battery, AI sensor and the rest â€” must be secured to the robot with #8-32 machine screws. A different thread count can damage the part. Lengths may vary.

Consumable kit â€” go ahead

  • Modify as you like within these rules.
  • Replacements you buy yourself must match the kit part's material, treatment or grade, and dimensions. A 1×4 can't become a 2×4 of the same volume.
  • Only the provided wire, snap-plug terminals and optional quick-disconnect terminals may conduct electricity â€” plus solder at the connections. The one exception: any provided metallic material may be part of a sensor circuit.
  • Team-supplied pennies may not be altered.
  • No packaging materials, such as the film covering the adhesive on a hook-and-loop strip.

Fabrication rules

What you can do to material, and what crosses the line.

Allowed

  • Any tools or machines may be used to make parts â€” but students are expected to be taught to use them and to be the ones operating them.
  • Metal, rubber and plastic may be heated and reformed.
  • Lubricants, for lubrication only. Contaminating the field or an opponent with lubricant is not allowed.
  • Solder your own wire connections, except where connectors are provided â€” those must be used without soldering to the connector. Solder is fine on consumable-kit bullet or quick-disconnect connectors.
  • Strings and the outer sheath of shock cord may be singed to stop fraying.
  • Markers, paint and printouts for visual information that doesn't help perform game tasks â€” labelling parts, taping a copy of the V5 port schematic to the machine.

Not allowed

  • No welding, brazing or structural soldering.
  • Metal, rubber and plastic may not be melted and re-cast.
  • Materials may not be changed chemically (Team Custom Parts are exempt from this one).
  • Nothing built before kickoff, and no parts reused from previous competitions. BEST personnel may permanently mark parts â€” scribing, drilling â€” to prevent reuse.
  • Paint or finish may not change the mechanical properties of what it's applied to. Its optical properties â€” colour and reflectivity â€” may be used functionally.
  • No strobe lights. Other lights are fine.

Decorations and cameras

Non-functional decorations are permitted only if they don't help the machine perform game tasks. The test: if you can remove it or cover it and the machine behaves the same, it's non-functional â€” and you may be asked to prove it. Decorations may use a separate power source such as a 9 V battery.

Video capture devices like a GoPro or phone are allowed under the decoration rules plus: display no larger than 6″ diagonal, screen off or covered, no transmitting or streaming, non-kit mounting hardware counts as part of the device, and BEST officials may ask for it to be removed at any time for any reason. Protect it yourself â€” BEST isn't responsible for damage.

Team Custom Parts

  • Four allowed, each fitting a 2″ × 4″ × 4″ cuboid.
  • Any uniform, homogeneous team-supplied material. Surface treatment on metals or magnets is fine â€” anodising on aluminium, nickel coating on a neodymium magnet.
  • Each part must be a single continuous piece of material in its operational state.
  • Start from basic raw stock: rectangular or cylindrical, if the final part keeps any of that original shape. Starting shape is irrelevant if the material was liquid during forming, or if the part is carved entirely from a solid block.
  • No kit parts inserted or embedded in a TCP. Kit screws or zip ties to attach it are fine.
  • No hazardous materials, no welding. Chemical change is allowed here.

Compliance

Every machine is inspected before the competition, and may be re-inspected at any point during it.

  • Random re-checks happen through the day at the referees' discretion. A non-compliant machine can't continue until it's fixed, and may be disqualified from matches it already played.
  • Machines may not leave the competition site between the compliance check and the start of the competition without approval.
  • Machines may be modified between matches, but must still meet every regulation afterwards and the compliance official must approve the changes before the next match.
  • No substitute machines.
  • Teams advancing to a championship may repair and functionally improve their machine, then get re-checked before that competition.

Multiple configurations

A machine may have several configurations â€” swappable arms, for instance. Each configuration must independently meet the size and weight limits and pass a compliance check.

The catch: the sum of all parts across all configurations can't exceed the quantities on the returnable and consumable kit lists, and the robot still can't exceed 24 pounds.

Also required to compete

Every student on the Game Day roster must complete the Student Participation Survey â€” verified at the compliance check â€” and every team member including teachers and mentors must be registered individually in the BEST National Registry. A compliant engineering notebook must be submitted a week before the competition. Pass compliance and you're guaranteed at least 25 robot performance points; fail and you get 0 and are ineligible for robot awards or advancement.

Three lines that catch teams out

The brain's on/off switch has to stay accessible and the battery has to be secured in a holder or mount — neither appears in Section 1.3. Nor does the requirement that sharp points and edges underneath the robot be taped over, which is a handling-safety check rather than a design rule. And detachable parts are not free weight: they go on the scale and into the cube with the machine.

What counts as a legal Team Custom Part

Section 1.3.2.4 gives you nine lines. The Team Custom Part Inspection Guide in Appendix A gives you the reasoning and a set of worked examples — this is what the inspector is going by.

“Uniform” is judged by eye

The threshold is deliberately low: if it looks uniform to the naked eye, it counts as uniform. Fibre-reinforced plastic passes, because the micro fibres are not visible. Foam rubber passes — aeration does not make a material non-uniform. Two-part epoxy mixed, poured and set passes, because it becomes one material.

Composite materials do not pass. The example given is fibre and glue — carbon or glass fibre with epoxy. The one exception is a surface treatment on metal or a magnet: anodising, or the nickel coating on a neodymium magnet.

Each part may use a different material

Nothing requires your four parts to match. Magnets, silicone, expanded foam, granite and wood are all fair game alongside the usual plastic and metal.

How it may be made

  • Carved from a solid block, or melted and reformed — so a 3-D printer is explicitly allowed for a plastic part
  • Metal may be cast; melted plastic may be injected into a mould
  • A machined “hog-out” from solid metal or plastic is fine
  • Melting is allowed. Welding is not. Chemical change is allowed here, unlike everywhere else on the robot
  • An existing CAD/STEP file may be used, student-created or publicly available — but coaches, teachers and mentors may not make those files for students, and the part itself must be fabricated during this season

Fitting the cuboid

You may be asked to take the part off the robot to measure it. A flexible part may be temporarily folded, coiled or knotted to fit for verification — it may flex, but it may not be reformed to meet the limit. “Single continuous piece” is strict: a normal hinge counts as three parts, and an item with thin ligaments that snap into several pieces in use is not allowed.

How judging works

  • Judges come from private and public sectors with technical and non-technical expertise, and serve on a rotation.
  • Notebook judging teams have 2 to 4 scorers who read notebooks in their entirety, no more than 5 notebooks per team of judges.
  • A component's score is the average of the individual judges' scores. Where a component has 3 or more judges, the hub may drop the lowest.
  • Teams get a copy of their score sheets within 3 days of their local competition, and within 10 days of the last regional championship date.
  • Scores among many teams frequently differ by fractions of a point.
  • Teams advancing to a championship may use judges' comments to improve, subject to the championship's own deadlines.

Asking a question that actually gets answered

The Q&A is the only sanctioned way to close any of these, and it runs during the build phase only — not on Game Day.

Read first

The rulebook's own advice is blunt: most questions are answered by reading the rules thoroughly. A question that quotes the section it is about, and says which two readings are in play, is far more likely to get a usable answer than “can we do X?”

Quote the conflict

Where two sections disagree, name both. “3.3.1.3.B says or, 3.6.2.I says and — which governs?” is a question the committee can answer in one line. It also tells them a revision may be needed.

Design for the strict reading

What was not verified

Four honest gaps. None of them is hidden anywhere else on this site.

The field drawings were never opened

The rulebook repeatedly defers to a separate document, the Byte to Bite Field Drawings, for exact sizes, fiducial placement and fiducial ID values. That document was not consulted in building this page.

So treat every dimension here as indicative. If you are machining to a measurement, get it from the drawings.

Only 2 of 18 figures were examined

The rulebook carries 18 numbered figures. Two were opened and read: the field overview and the dining room. The station drawings — vegetable tray, fruit vines, fridge, breadbox, water station, drone, warming shelf, balance buffet, dish starting area — were not.

Anything shown only in a drawing, and never written in the prose, will be missing from this page.

The kit lists were read by eye

Appendix B is four image-only pages, so the 142 line items were transcribed by reading the rendered pages rather than extracting text. Group totals were confirmed against the source — 38 returnable, 82 consumable, 22 team-provided — and items were spot-checked.

But no character-by-character comparison was possible. Check a part number against the real kit list before you order anything.

Open Questions are readings, not rulings

The 14 items on that tab are interpretations of ambiguous text. They are quoted and cited so you can judge them, but several could be resolved the other way by a referee or a Q&A answer — in which case the answer governs and this page is wrong.

Do not build a strategy on one without asking the Q&A first.

The thirteen pages with no text layer

Worth explaining, because it caused the one real gap in this guide.

Thirteen of the rulebook's 89 pages are scanned images rather than text: pages 36, 53–62, 65 and 89. Automated checking cannot see them at all. Early drafts of this page were checked against extracted text, so those pages reported clean while actually being invisible — and the whole of Appendix A, the compliance checklist and the Team Custom Part inspection guide, was simply missing from this site.

All thirteen have since been opened and read as images. Appendix A is now in Robot Limits and Appendix I in Awards & Judging. It is flagged here because it is exactly the kind of gap that a confident-looking verification process can hide.

If you are checking this page yourself

Every claim carries its section number. Open the rulebook to that section and read it — that is faster than trusting anyone's summary, this one included. For anything in an appendix, look at the page rather than searching the PDF, because search will not find text that is part of an image.

What this page does not replace

Five documents this guide summarises, references or simply does not contain.

The rulebook itself

2026 BEST Robotics Classic Competition Rules v3.2. The authority for everything here. Later revisions supersede both it and this page.

Byte to Bite Field Drawings

Exact dimensions, fiducial sizes, placements and ID values. Not summarised here at all.

The Team Custom Part Guide

A separate document with fuller explanation and examples. Appendix A's inspection guide is summarised here; the standalone guide is not.

Your hub's Awards and Judging Logistics

Published in the National Registry. It sets your judging criteria, deadlines, formats and which choice awards are offered. Nothing here substitutes for it.

The online Q&A

Answers at game.bestrobotics.org/qna are an extension of the rules and go to every team. They can change what is correct after this page was written.

A referee

On Game Day, referees have ultimate authority and no protests are allowed (1.4.1.A). Questions go to a Team Advocate, never to a referee or scorekeeper.

Order of authority, shortest version

The referee's call on the day, then the rulebook and any posted Q&A answers, then your hub's own documents — and this page a long way after all of them. It is a study aid written by readers of the rulebook, not by BEST Robotics, and it has no standing in a match.
